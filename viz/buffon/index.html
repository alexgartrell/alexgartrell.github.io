The axis is logarithmic. The typical error shrinks as 1 ÷ √n, so each extra correct decimal costs about 100× more needles. Longer needles cross more often and converge a little faster.

Why this works

Describe a dropped needle by two random numbers: the distance d from its centre to the nearest line, uniform between 0 and t/2, and its angle θ to the lines, uniform between 0 and π/2. It crosses a line exactly when d ≤ (ℓ/2)·sin θ. That region takes up 2ℓ / (πt) of all the possible (d, θ) pairs, so that is the chance of a crossing. The π sneaks in through the angle.

Count the crossings and solve for π: π ≈ 2ℓ × needles ÷ (t × crossings). Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon, posed the problem in 1733 and published the answer in 1777. In 1901 Mario Lazzarini reported 3,408 needles giving 355⁄113 = 3.1415929, a result so good that it is widely believed he stopped at a convenient moment.

How it works

Every peg is a coin flip: right with probability p, left otherwise. After n rows a ball's bin is just its number of rights, so bin k collects a share C(n, k) · pᵏ · (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ of the balls. The C(n, k) counts the routes to that bin, which is why the peg triangle is Pascal’s triangle in disguise.

A sum of n independent flips has mean np and standard deviation √(np(1 − p)). The central limit theorem says that such a sum looks more and more like a normal curve as n grows, whatever p is. Tilt the board with a small n and the pile is lopsided; add rows and the bell comes back.
